# Reference review of past Erdős quant-finance projects

Prepared 2 November 2026 for the Fall 2026 Erdős Institute project "Dynamic Treasury hedging of Agency MBS."

## 1. Purpose and method

This review gathers evidence from eleven Erdős Institute quant-finance boot-camp projects that the project folder labels "previous award-winning projects," together with the Fall 2025 Treasury hedging project, to guide the design of the Agency MBS hedging project. For each project I read the local slides and summaries, read the cloned GitHub repository without executing its code, opened the Erdős project page, and checked headline numbers against saved notebook outputs and result files. These examples document recurring practices, and they cannot serve as a causal experiment about why judges awarded prizes, because the sample is small, preselected, and mostly unverified as award winners.

### 5.1 Two claims checked

**(a) Bubble analysis and timing.** The project reports a timing null. Its slides and summary answer "Can we time crashes? No" and attribute the month-level AUC of 0.685 to an implicit clock (a month counter alone scores 0.931) and to cross-episode level differences. Inside fixed pre-peak windows, the 14 volatility and SEC features reach a 5-fold AUC of 0.608 and single features 0.43–0.53, which the author calls chance. The same slide gives a 95% chance band of [0.42, 0.58] for n = 174, so 0.608 sits slightly above the author's own band, and the figure appears in none of the repository's result files. The author's finding is the absence of timing predictability. "Weak timing predictability" claims more than the author does, although one unreproduced number leaves room for a weak signal.

**(b) Amaris Williams and the volatility baseline.** The slides report $57.26 per 100 contracts for both arms and conclude "No Evidence Against the Null Hypothesis." In `2_Compare_to_Heston.ipynb`, cell 15 stores the LSTM result in a misspelled variable (`proftit = heston_call_price + call_profit_hedged`) and then prints `profit`, which still holds the rolling-volatility result from cell 13. The LSTM arm's profit was never displayed, so the identical figures are an artifact. The test is also one 252-day S&P 500 path with one at-the-money call, and notebook 1 never compares LSTM forecast error with the rolling-volatility forecast error. The evidence supports only "no advantage demonstrated," and it cannot support "no material advantage."

## 6. The 2025 Treasury hedging project in detail

### 6.1 Design

- **Question.** How many IEF and TLT shares to short against a Treasury ladder so that its key-rate DV01 vector is neutral, and how much risk that removes historically and in simulation.
- **Portfolio.** Three 3%-coupon bonds (2y, 5y, and 10y) with $100 face each: $300 face, $287.94 on 20 Oct 2025, and DV01 of $0.1439 per bp (`2_analytics` cell 8).
- **Data.** FRED DGS2, DGS5, DGS10, and DGS30 with IEF and TLT daily closes from 2 Jan 2018 to 20 Oct 2025, 2,035 rows (`4_hedge_real_data` cell 0). Yahoo returned rate-limit errors and the Stooq fallback supplied the ETF closes (`1_fetch_data` cell 3).
- **Method.** CMT yields treated as zero rates with linear interpolation, KRDs from 1 bp bumps at 2, 5, 10, and 30 years, ETF KRDs from OLS of daily ETF returns on four yield changes over a trailing 504-business-day window (minimum 252 observations), a least-squares hedge, and monthly rebalancing (`4_hedge_real_data` cell 4). Monte Carlo: Vasicek fit to the 10y yield (a = 0.64, b = 4.21%, σ = 0.96%), PCA level and slope (86.8% and 11.5% of variance), and 500 one-year paths per model (`5_what_if` cells 3–7).
- **Comparators.** Unhedged only in the backtest. A TLT-only DV01 hedge appears for one date (`3_hedge_design` cell 9).
- **Validation.** Trailing-window estimation, a by-year table, a 1 bp cost variant, and the Monte Carlo.

### 6.3 How the backtest P&L is built

`4_hedge_real_data` cell 4 reprices the same three constant-maturity bonds on each day's curve, so the unhedged daily P&L is the change in that price and the cumulative P&L equals the change in ladder value. The bonds never age, and the P&L contains no coupon income, roll-down, pull-to-par, or financing. The hedge leg is minus the hedge units times the daily change in ETF share prices. From the cumulative P&L plot (cell 6) and the final value of $287.94, the ladder was worth about $310 in January 2018, about $343 at the 2020 peak, and about $270 in October 2023. The −73.06 drawdown is therefore a dollar fall of roughly 21% from the peak, which is plausible for a price-only ladder with a duration near 5 through the 2020–2023 rate rise. It looks implausible because the summary and slides print it and the other dollar statistics as percentages, and because the portfolio is a synthetic bond ladder that the ETFs hedge. The two series are almost identical through 2018 because the 252-observation minimum delays the first hedge to 31 Dec 2018 (cell 6 plot, 2018 variance reduction 0.6%).

### 6.4 Agreement across summary, slides, poster, and notebooks

## 7. Lessons mapped to the seven practices

| Practice | Exemplars that show it (and counterexamples) | Implication for the MBS project |
|---|---|---|
| 1. A recognizable financial decision | Avellaneda–Stoikov (quote to offload inventory), Dynamic Fear Hedging (hedge an equity position with VXX), Kay (choose v₀ for a vega hedge), 2025 project (size an ETF overlay) | Open with the PM decision: how much Treasury DV01 to short per $100mm of Agency MBS on each rebalance date, using tradable futures or ETFs |
| 2. One central idea with a visible mechanism | Kou (a jump-intensity sweep widens hedged tails), Avellaneda–Stoikov (the reservation price skews quotes), Bubble Analysis (leverage vs mania regimes) | Show negative convexity directly with rolling empirical MBS duration plotted against refinancing incentive, so viewers see why a fixed hedge over-hedges in rallies |
| 3. A meaningful simple comparator | Bubble Analysis (naive vol-ratio floor), CDS (challenger hierarchy), Avellaneda–Stoikov (symmetric quotes), Dynamic Fear Hedging (static vs dynamic). Counterexample: 2025 project (unhedged only) | Rank unhedged, static duration, simple updating (rolling-regression), and refinancing-aware hedges, with the simple updating hedge as the bar to clear |
| 4. A quantified practical consequence in PM units | Avellaneda–Stoikov (dollar mean and SD), Dynamic Fear Hedging (drawdown in percent), Heston (vol points). Counterexamples: 2025 project (dollars printed as percent), Kay ("$1M" label on a different base) | Report monthly hedging error in bp of NAV, worst month, and turnover cost in bp/yr, per $100mm |
| 5. Validation that matches the claim | Bubble Analysis (pre-peak data, permutation test, walk-forward), Heston (verification before use), CDS (leave-one-out). Counterexamples: Smile (same-day holdout), Dynamic Fear Hedging (full-sample weight), 2025 project (Monte Carlo built on the hedge model), Williams (one path) | Walk-forward with lagged inputs only, identical windows and costs for all hedges, block-bootstrap intervals on the improvement, and regime splits (2020–21 refinancing wave, 2022 extension) |
| 6. An honest finding that remains interesting | Bubble Analysis (timing null and walk-forward failure stated on slides), Heston (2.8× against the paper's 10–16×), 2025 poster ("Where it breaks") | Report the size of the improvement over the simple updating hedge with its interval, and show the conditions (coupon moneyness, rally months) where refinancing information matters most |
| 7. A reproducible visual story | Bubble Analysis (derived data shipped, scripts regenerate every result), Heston (one executed notebook on a cached snapshot), Dynamic Fear Hedging (notebooks save every figure and table). Counterexamples: Volatility Complex (no outputs, schematic chart), Avellaneda–Stoikov (unseeded runs), 2025 project (missing data files, mismatched README) | Numbered notebooks on cached data, fixed seeds, one figure per claim, and a check script that recomputes every number in the summary and slides |
